What China's renewable decade actually teaches Australia

China is running the largest and fastest energy build-out in human history — and, unusually for any country, it publishes the plan. Seven lessons for the NEM, read from the source documents.

Two documents now define China's decade: the 15th Five-Year Plan for renewable energy (2026–2030), unveiled on 23 July 2026 — the first dedicated five-year plan China has ever issued for renewables — and the 2035 NDC announced at the UN on 24 September 2025, China's first absolute emissions-reduction pledge. Together they commit to roughly 3,500 GW of renewables by 2030 and 3,600 GW of wind and solar alone by 2035. China's recent build rate — 360 GW added in 2024, about 315 GW of solar in 2025 — is already ahead of the pace those targets require. Analysts widely read them as a floor, not a ceiling.

Why this matters to an Australian developer: China is the reference class for how fast solar-plus-storage can be built, the source of the world's cheapest equipment, and — through its own struggles with curtailment and pricing reform — a preview of the market-design problems the NEM is entering now.

Hitting these needs about 160 GW of new wind and solar a year — below China's current run-rate. The more interesting move is qualitative: the plan requires wind to deliver at least 11% and solar at least 6% "reliable capacity" by 2030, with wind and solar covering more than 20% of peak demand in critical periods. China is now regulating firmness, not just capacity — the same direction Australia's Capacity Investment Scheme points.

The outer marker: 2035

The NDC pledges an economy-wide emissions cut of 7–10% from peak by 2035, and 3,600 GW of wind and solar — six times 2020 levels. The expert reading (CREA's Lauri Myllyvirta among others): a set of targets prepared by a conservative bureaucracy — a floor. Reaching 3,600 GW needs only ~200 GW a year; China added 360 GW in 2024 alone. The practical consequence for us: plan on China overshooting, which means equipment prices keep falling through the Solaris procurement windows of 2028–2030.

The geography lesson

The Chinese Academy of Sciences assessment that anchors this research maps where the resource actually is: the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau holds 45.6% of China's solar potential and 38.5% of its wind potential. That geography produced the megabase playbook — concentrate gigawatt-scale solar and storage where the resource is best, then move it over ultra-high-voltage lines to coastal load centres, thousands of kilometres away.

Australia's translation is more favourable. Our best-resource zones sit hundreds of kilometres from load, not thousands — and retiring coal sites provide grid nodes China had to build from scratch. Same logic, better map.

Seven lessons

  1. Speed is a policy choice, not a physical limit. 315 GW in one year proves the constraint is coordination, not technology.
  2. Firmness is the new metric everywhere. China's 6%-firm-solar rule and the CIS point the same way: the product is dependable power, not megawatt-hours. Solaris is designed storage-first for this reason.
  3. Co-locate storage by design, not afterthought. China made it policy — including the world's largest vanadium flow batteries (Dalian, 800 MWh; Jimusar, 1 GWh beside a 1 GW solar plant). The NEM's negative midday prices are teaching the same lesson the hard way.
  4. Hydrogen: big multiple, small base. Even China targets only 2 Mt by 2030, from 0.25. E-fuels are optionality, never foundation.
  5. Equipment deflation continues. Targets that outrun demand keep factories oversupplied — TOPCon modules at US$0.113/W FOB, battery systems near US$75/kWh, and a structural capex tailwind ahead.
  6. Merchant risk is universal. A year after "Document 136" moved new renewables to market pricing, only about half of China's provinces have finalised the rules, and generators face real revenue uncertainty. Independent validation of a revenue-floor-first strategy.
  7. The megabase logic is our logic, with a better map. Best resource, strong grid node, storage — Australia's coal-exit geography delivers the combination with far less new transmission.

What we're watching

  • Whether the remaining provinces finalise Document 136's mechanism auctions — and where the price floors settle. China's merchant turbulence is a preview of ours.
  • Whether CIS tenders start rewarding duration beyond four hours — the opening long-duration storage, vanadium included, is waiting for.
  • Whether China's 2026 additions confirm the overshoot — the equipment-price tailwind our 2028–2030 procurement assumptions lean on.
